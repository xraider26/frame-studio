## Assessment

Useful as a beginner production reference, especially for camera intent, framing, lighting, character sheets, and shot planning. It is not a demonstrated automated business. The larger advertising blueprints repeat the same frameworks, contain reference/timing/continuity errors, and often overload short clips. Growth and product claims are frequently unsupported. For our storytelling work, use the directing principles and repair the examples before generation.

## Short-film blueprint audit

All 173 pages were covered. Strong reusable structure: canonical characters, locations and props; uploaded-reference maps; visible headcount; staging; timed action; assigned speakers; opening/ending constraints; and persistent object states.

Concrete defects make automatic copy-paste unreliable:

- Shot 08 closes the driver door and retrieves through the window; Shot 11 later says the door was opened before frame one.
- Shot 09 says a character chews continuously and then explicitly stops; arm-position instructions contradict the required gesture.
- Shot 14 is labelled one take but directs a hard cut between different setups.
- Shot 15 changes beard, glasses and costume descriptions; the continuing location also changes.
- Shot 26 demands fixed rider/car relative positions while having the rider catch up and pull away.
- Shots 28–29 move a passenger between seats without a transition.
- Shot 46's reference header and prompt body number different assets. Shots 51 and 58 also disagree on attachment mappings.
- Shot 57 changes bike colour from the earlier specification.
- Shot 65 is labelled 8 seconds but describes action through 9 seconds; Shot 70 is labelled 10 seconds but runs through 10.4 seconds.

Generation order is not necessarily edit order. Preserve distinct IDs for scenes, edit shots, generation jobs, and chosen takes. Do not impose one focal length per location or one camera-relative shadow direction on every angle.

## Production playbook

### 1. Establish the brief

Record audience, niche, platform, viewer promise, story premise, format, target runtime, aspect ratio, visual references, factual requirements and available generation credits. Do not assume a niche from past projects. Treat audience predictions as hypotheses to test.

Deliverable: a selected concept with a clear opening and payoff, plus the concrete resource limit for the pilot.

### 2. Write and time the story

Draft a few genuinely different hooks. Select one that the story can pay off. Build opening, development and resolution; each scene must add new information or emotion. Separate narration, dialogue, visual action and captions.

For narration-led work, make a scratch read before generating visuals. Use measured speaking time, pauses and breathing room. Recalculate beats to the selected runtime; do not transplant a 40-second ad timeline into a 15-second video. Distinguish source facts from dramatisation and illustrative marketing examples.

Deliverable: timed script and edit beats summing to the target runtime.

### 3. Lock references and state

Create a clean canonical portrait, then check all views in the character sheet. Record immutable identity, intentional costume variants, location layout, prop appearance, voice direction and material treatment. Assign stable asset IDs and versions.

Track mutable state per shot: wet/dry clothes, damage, held objects, door position, passenger seat, emotional state, light/time, movement direction. Track the actual window/lamp position in the environment, then derive its camera-relative direction for each setup.

Build a per-generation map from actual upload slot to asset ID. Named tags from another app must not be assumed to work in Flow. Every invoked asset must exist and be attached when required.

Deliverable: reference registry, location sketch and before/after state ledger.

### 4. Design individual shot cards

Use subject + environment + camera/composition + light + mood + optional style for still images. For motion, concentrate on what changes: opening state + primary action + one camera move with speed + ending state + timing + relevant sound.

Vary wide, medium and close shots according to story purpose. Specify foreground, midground, background and lead room when useful. Simpler movement suits close faces. A locked camera is a valid choice. Use a deliberate end composition; avoid default aimless drift.

Keep one primary action and one main move per dependable generation. Separate difficult transitions, multiple locations and dense dialogue into editable shots. Treat complex in-camera transitions as experiments. Generated duration and retained edit duration are different fields.

### 5. Check the actual Flow interface

Inspect available model, generation mode, aspect ratio, duration, audio, reference controls, displayed credit cost and export options in the user's account. Choose text-to-video for loose exploration, image-to-video when opening identity/composition matters, and start/end images only when supported and useful.

Map the creative specification to observed controls. Do not assume course-specific durations, multi-shot features, reference counts or settings. Do all preparation before requesting any missing budget decision. Existing user authorisation governs execution.

Deliverable: verified settings and a reviewable pilot shot; no generation is claimed by this report.

### 6. Generate and retain evidence

Generate a representative pilot, inspect it, then expand within the established limit. Keep prompts, settings, references and results together. Suggested operating default: initial attempt plus two targeted revisions per shot, subject to the actual budget; this is our proposal, not a course rule.

On failure, name the defect and change the smallest useful constraint. Simplify or redesign after repeated failure. Do not continue spending on blind retries.

### 7. Quality gate

Before submission, check:

- All asset IDs resolve; upload maps match prompt text; headcount and active speaker agree.
- Timings fit clip duration; dialogue fits its beat; edit durations sum correctly.
- Opening state matches the preceding ending state, or a transition explains the change.
- Required actions do not contradict negatives or “constant” state instructions.
- Light sources, wardrobe variants, prop colours, seats and screen direction remain coherent.

After generation, watch the full clip and inspect beginning, middle and end. Check identity, anatomy, contact/physics, geometry, props/text, camera intent, background, lighting, audio and lip sync. Compare edits with their original. A clean clip must still serve the story.

A chosen final frame can anchor the next clip when supported, but continuity must be checked again. Use B-roll or an editorial cut for a local problem when appropriate; do not hide a story-critical continuity failure.

### 8. Edit, mix and package

Set aspect ratio before assembly. Trim for the story rather than retaining every generated second. Keep speaker audio under listener cutaways and animate only the intended speaker. Add ambience, action-synchronised foley, final voice, purposeful music and corrected captions. Match colour between shots. Add exact end-card text in editing where possible.

Review the final exported file at its intended viewing size. Verify runtime, framing, captions, sound, beginning/end and transitions. Use the actual licenses for selected audio/assets.

Prepare titles, thumbnail and description around the same promise as the opening. Publishing requires the user's publishing instruction; none is performed here.

### 9. Learn from the pilot

Track total credits, cost per usable shot, attempts, production time and defect type. When performance data exists, inspect retention, completion, shares and audience response; use impressions and click-through when relevant. Diagnose story demand separately from execution quality. Change one major variable at a time for useful comparisons.

No background task is configured. Browser work should not be assumed to continue after the active conversation ends.
